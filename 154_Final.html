For the 2019 and 2022 data I chose to examine variables pertaining to health status, health expenditures, and number of visits to the ER and physician’s office. I thought it would be interesting to analyze and compare if there were any differences in these variables to determine whether any significant change occurred. According to the data, mean health expenditures increased by about 570 dollars and the standard deviation increased, which suggests greater variability in healthcare expenditure habits. Prescription expenditures also saw an increase in the mean of about 410 dollars, with two times the increase in its standard deviation suggesting higher variability in prescription costs. For er and office visits, both variables saw a relative decline which suggests a small reduction in emergency care and visitation usage. I believe the data is a reflection of the effects of Covid 19, as the higher expenditures for both healthcare and prescriptions reflect the increasing healthcare costs post-Covid as more people sought to get more treatment and more medications. The increased variability may suggest disparities in spending, such as some individuals requiring more intensive care, while others may have reduced healthcare interactions. Office visits and ER visits both declined from 2019 to 2022. This could be due to an increase in people relying less on in person visits and attempting to manage their health in other ways post-pandemic. Many individuals may have also stopped visiting for cases when they feel it is not urgent enough. This shift reflects a change in behavior from patients. This change in behavior could also be seen through the self-reported health status. The slight decrease in poor health could be attributed to people taking better care of themselves post-pandemic. In addition, the drop in excellent health relative to the rise in good health status may be reflective of shifting knowledge about personal health. Covid 19 may have either changed perceptions about what “excellent health” really is, or people may have utilized services that lead them to understand how healthy they are. Overall, the data allows me to see many things. First is that spending went up–possibly due to higher healthcare costs and post-pandemic medical needs. Second is that healthcare utilization declined, which may reflect changes in healthcare patient behavior, such as avoiding unnecessary visits. And third is that health status perceptions shifted, with fewer people reporting excellent health but also fewer in poor health—suggesting a reconfiguration of what “good health” meant post-pandemic.

I decided to examine the data through income groups by filtering and grouping accordingly with the family income variable. By combining the datasets to get more robust observations, I find that by regressing log health expenditures on age and health status variables, that age is statistically significantly influencing health expenditures positively across all income groups–which is expected. The effect is slightly stronger for lower income groups (0.057 and 0.063 increase per 1 year of age versus 0.061 and 0.051 increase per 1 year of age) which suggests that aging might contribute more significantly to health expenditures among those who have lower incomes. The health status variables all significantly influence log health expenditures positively across all income groups as well, however the poor health status has the largest impact on increasing health expenditures across all groups as expected. To dive deeper, the coefficients for poor health are the highest among the group below the poverty line (1.968) and the lowest in the higher income group (1.483). There is a clear trend where the impact of poor health on health expenditure decreases as income increases, as the coefficients range from 1.968, 1.768, 1.536, to 1.483. This suggests again that lower income groups may experience more significant rises in health expenditures when they have poor health, possibly because they have fewer preventative care options, or they rely more on expensive emergency care. For good health, it is seen that there is a statistically significant positive increase as well for expenditures, however the magnitude of effect is smaller in comparison to the poor health. Once again, I find that the coefficient in this case is the highest among the lowest income group (0.846), and slightly lower among middle and high income groups (0.699 and 0.731). The constant term suggests that baseline spending is higher for wealthier groups than it is for lower income groups. In addition, wealthier groups seem to spend just more money overall, even in better health. Overall from the regression I find that health expenditures increase with age, and the effect is slightly more impactful in lower income groups. I also find that poor health significantly increases health expenditures for all income groups, but especially for lower income groups, and that higher income groups have a higher baseline level of health expenditures. The full picture analysis that I conclude with is that lower income groups experience bigger financial burdens when in poor health, where in contrast wealthier groups tend to have higher overall spending regardless of health status. Since poor health seems to disproportionately affect spending for the lower income population, I believe that this calls for the need of policies to help ease the financial burden of this vulnerable group, such as subsidies or Medicaid expansion.

# aggregate the data to calculate mean er visits
agg <- aggregate(data = meps_combined, er_visits ~ age, FUN = "mean")

# square age
agg <- agg %>% 
  mutate(age_squared = age^2)  

# create the scatterplot 
ggplot(agg, aes(x = age, y = er_visits)) +
  geom_point(color = "blue", size = 2) +  
  geom_smooth(
    method = "lm",  
    formula = y ~ x + I(x^2),  
    color = "red", 
    se = TRUE  
  ) +  
  labs(
    title = "Mean ER Visits by Age (Quadratic Fit)",
    x = "Age",
    y = "Mean ER Visits"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()

In this case I plotted a scatterplot of age on the x axis and the mean visits to the ER on the Y axis. Through this plot I found a non-linear, quadratic relationship between age and mean ER visits. This suggests that ER utilization may have remianed relatively low and somewhat stable for most of people’s adulthood, however visitation sharply increases once people get even older. Essentially, the probability of an ER visit accelerates significantly as people age rather than remaining at a constant rate. This suggests that older individuals around the range of 70 have a higher demand for emergency care likely due to increased illnesses, chronic conditions, and more frequent hospitalizations that may come from events such as heart attacks. This could suggest the need for increased preventative care systems and such things to reduce the need of ER dependence as people grow older.
